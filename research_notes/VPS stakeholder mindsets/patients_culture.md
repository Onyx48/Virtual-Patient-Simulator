# How MSK Patients and SP Actors in Singapore, Hong Kong and Japan Talk and Behave in History-Taking

Note on scope: this was a time-limited pass (about 12 searches and fetches). Several of the most relevant sources are older than the 2015 cut-off. They are kept here but marked [pre-2015]. A lot of everyday speech detail, such as Singlish idioms, was not covered in any citable source. Those items are listed under Gaps and were not invented.

## 1. How patients describe pain and symptoms (Singapore, Hong Kong, Japan)

### Takeaway
Patients in all three places use traditional medicine alongside physiotherapy, and in Chinese communities TCM ideas of "balance" shape how people explain their pain. Japanese patients often describe pain with sound-symbolic words (ideophones). I found no peer-reviewed corpus of Singlish or Cantonese-English pain talk in clinics.

### Cited Findings
- SGH RCT (204 patients with chronic non-specific low back pain, age up to 75, recruited Mar 2021 to Jan 2023) compared physiotherapy, tuina from registered TCM physicians, and both. Pain at 2 months was comparable across arms. At 5 months the physio and combined arms did better on walking, sitting and lifting. SGH notes that some people visit both physiotherapists and TCM physicians "hoping to get better faster". — [SGH news](https://www.sgh.com.sg/news/patient-care/tcm-tuina-therapy-as-effective-as-physiotherapy-for-short-term-relief-of-lower-back-pain-sgh-study)
- Quotes from that SGH piece that are useful for persona design. TCM practitioner: "The tendency is that you will have to come back to me." / "If it's chronic pain, I can solve your pain but the relief (lasts) maybe a few months." SGH physio Celia Tan: tuina patients have "the tendency for them to feel that the pain is coming back"; the aim of physio is "to get them to be responsible for their treatment". A 63-year-old participant called the combined approach "the best of both worlds". — [SGH news](https://www.sgh.com.sg/news/patient-care/tcm-tuina-therapy-as-effective-as-physiotherapy-for-short-term-relief-of-lower-back-pain-sgh-study)
- SGH Pain Management Centre survey of 210 chronic pain patients: TCM was the most common complementary therapy (68%). For upper-limb pain, patients most often used tui na, massage or a TCM practitioner, and most felt it helped. [pre-2015, 2013] — [BVS record](https://search.bvsalud.org/gim/resource/en/wpr-305736)
- Hong Kong: many people use TCM and Western medicine at the same time and choose which to see by type of illness. Bone-setting is one of the named TCM forms. — [HKU repository](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/139870/1/Content.pdf)
- Hong Kong primary care: patients seeing TCM practitioners most often presented with musculoskeletal disorders, and these were more chronic and severe. — [HKU repository](https://repository.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/252227/1/content.pdf)
- People from all walks of life in Hong Kong visit bone-setters (跌打) for skeletal problems. The bone-setters use herbal medicines and manual therapy. This is an informal, older student source. — [CUHK Varsity](https://varsity.com.cuhk.edu.hk/varsity/0011/pf_tida/tida.htm)
- Mainland China LBP beliefs study (2020): TCM ideas centred on "balance" were evident in how contemporary Chinese people understand low back pain. — [BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2020](https://bmcmusculoskeletdisord.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12891-020-03500-1)
- Japan: in a Pfizer Japan 2016 nationwide survey, 80% of chronic-pain patients said they often or sometimes use ideophones to describe pain (e.g. zuki-zuki throbbing, piri-piri tingling, shiku-shiku dull). This figure is reported second-hand. — [Curtin/UniSZA record, "Japanese interpretations of 'pain' and the use of psychomimes"](https://cendekia.unisza.edu.my/neuaxis-e/Record/curtin-20.500.11937-46639/Description)
- Hong Kong code-mixing: Cantonese is the base language, with English words embedded within sentences. Speakers switch mostly to fill vocabulary gaps (technical or medical terms), and the topic and the listener's age and education affect how much they mix. These findings come from general and workplace studies, not clinical ones. — [PolyU thesis](https://theses.lib.polyu.edu.hk/handle/200/3446); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code-switching_in_Hong_Kong)
- Singapore elderly: an ethnographic study of 30 older adults and 5 providers found significant communication barriers in medical encounters, and language shift between generations is disrupting family communication. — [NTU event](https://www.ntu.edu.sg/soh/news-events/events/detail/2021/09/17/lms/where-have-all-my-languages-gone-aging-and-the-changing-multilingual-linguistic-ecology); [Taylor & Francis chapter](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/oa-edit/10.4324/9781003125815-11/languages-gone-aging-changing-multilingual-linguistic-ecology-ng-bee-chin-francesco-cavallaro)
- Tamil-speaking elderly in Singapore rely heavily on untrained interpreters, mainly relatives, and on their own memory. The interpreting was seen as inadequate. — [NTU FYP](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/ntu-10356%2F73494/Description); also [NTU 2023 thesis on family/staff interpreters](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/ntu-10356%2F168168)

### Inferences
- A realistic Singapore or Hong Kong persona should often have already tried TCM (tuina, bone-setter, medicated oils or plasters) before physio, may still be using it at the same time, and may not mention it unless asked.
- Older personas should sometimes come with a family member who interprets or answers for them. Their English may be limited or mixed with Mandarin, dialects, Malay or Tamil.
- In Hong Kong, Cantonese with English nouns dropped in ("my back 好痛, 係 lower back") fits the base-language pattern. In Japan, ideophones plus vague location are realistic.

### Gaps
- I found no academic source documenting Singlish pain vocabulary ("pain pain", "sore", "aching", "heaty", "angin"/wind, "can or not"). It is plausible from general knowledge but uncited.
- No Reddit threads were retrievable, and there was no Singapore-specific study on sinseh use before physio.
- I found nothing citable on Malay (bomoh, urut) or Indian (ayurvedic) MSK practices in Singapore.

## 2. Health beliefs and yellow flags (fear of movement, rest, passive care, family, work, cost)

### Takeaway
There is evidence of stronger fear-avoidance and more negative back-pain beliefs among Singaporean and Taiwanese Chinese than among Australians, and of fear-avoidance as a valid construct in Hong Kong. The passive-care expectation shows up indirectly: TCM gives quick relief that does not last, so patients come back. There is little direct data on family, work or cost.

### Cited Findings
- Female healthcare students in Australia, Taiwan and Singapore (n=382): the Singaporean and Taiwanese Chinese students held more negative beliefs about the future consequences of low back pain and more fear-avoidant beliefs about physical activity. [pre-2015, 2009; students, not patients] — [ECU](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworks/549)
- The Fear-Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire has been validated in Hong Kong Chinese neck-pain patients. [pre-2015] — [HKMJ 2009](https://www.hkmj.org/node/7632)
- A Hong Kong adaptation of the Knee OA Fears and Beliefs Questionnaire (n=241) argues that fears and beliefs affect exercise adherence. — [MDPI Healthcare 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/12/3/310/html)
- Hong Kong limb-fracture patients avoided painkillers because they feared serious side effects, and these beliefs shaped how they coped. [pre-2015] — [PolyU](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/the-pain-experience-and-beliefs-of-chinese-patients-who-have-sust-2/)
- In the SGH trial, physio did better long-term, which the authors attribute to home exercise. Clinicians frame tuina as quick relief that brings patients back. — [SGH news](https://www.sgh.com.sg/news/patient-care/tcm-tuina-therapy-as-effective-as-physiotherapy-for-short-term-relief-of-lower-back-pain-sgh-study)
- Singapore physio "myths" blog (promotional, weak): rest is commonly believed to beat active rehab. — [Physio & Sole](https://physioandsole.com.sg/blog/7-common-physiotherapy-myths-singaporeans-still-believe/)
- Singapore physiotherapists ordered radiographs for acute whiplash more often than Queensland physios. This suggests a local culture of expecting imaging. — [search summary of Singapore vs Queensland survey; original not fetched]

### Inferences
- Realistic yellow flags for personas include: "the doctor said rest first", fear that bending will "make the disc worse", wanting a scan, wanting a machine or massage rather than exercise, avoiding painkillers, and relatives telling them not to move.

### Gaps
- I found no study quantifying cost concerns, MC (medical certificate) or work-culture pressures, or family decision-making in MSK rehab in SG, HK or JP.
- No Japanese yellow-flag data was found in this pass.

## 3. Reactions to sensitive questions (bowel/bladder, weight loss, mental health), deference and face

### Takeaway
Stigma and face-saving push Chinese patients to report physical rather than psychological symptoms and to disclose less. The evidence on somatization is contested. Nothing specific was found on bladder or bowel red-flag questions.

### Cited Findings
- Depressed Chinese patients may use physical complaints to take on the patient role without the stigma of mental illness. [pre-2015, 2001] — [Am J Psychiatry](https://ajp.psychiatryonline.org/cgi/content/full/158/6/857)
- Stigmatized patients may stress fatigue, sleep or appetite problems because they fear how they will be seen if they raise psychological symptoms. — [Springer 2023 review](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40120-023-00471-y)
- "Saving face" discourages disclosing emotions or psychological problems. — [eCALD resource](https://resources.ecald.com/working-with-asian-mental-health-clients-supplementary-resource/section-ii/cultural-issues-to-be-aware-of-when-working-with-asian-clients-in-general)
- Chinese people self-disclose less than Americans, and East Asian cultures discourage excessive speech. — [UBC blog summarising research](https://blogs.ubc.ca/psychology/?p=318)
- A counterpoint: another cross-cultural study suggests that high rates of somatic complaints in depression occur regardless of culture. — [search summary, Bohrium record](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/649ea310d68f896efa19a6d1)
- Japanese chronic-pain inpatients (n=49, Rorschach) showed neurotic, depressive and hypochondriac features plus defensive tendencies. [pre-2015, 2010] — [PMC3016376](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3016376/)
- A claim that Japanese heritage is linked with lower pain response (Komiyama et al. 2007), tied to stoicism, could not be verified first-hand. — [UniSA handout](https://lo.unisa.edu.au/mod/resource/view.php?id=1721614)

### Inferences
- Personas should answer mood questions with physical words ("can't sleep", "tired") and open up only after rapport is built or the question is normalised. They may hesitate or give a short "no lah" or "大丈夫です" (it's fine) to continence questions. Elderly patients may defer to the "doctor" and avoid contradicting them.

### Gaps
- I found no study on SG, HK or JP patient responses to cauda equina screening, weight-loss questions or modesty in physio.
- Gaman, enryo and Japanese understatement in MSK consultations are not covered by any reliable source found. Two blog pages making specific claims were rejected as unverifiable.

## 4. How SP actors are trained; what makes portrayals realistic or fake

### Takeaway
ASPE defines an SP as someone trained to portray a patient realistically and repeatably, with the degree of standardization set to suit the purpose. Recognised portrayal errors include giving too much information unprompted and inconsistency between performances. Rich character background improves how well a portrayal holds together.

### Cited Findings
- ASPE Standards of Best Practice (2017): an SP is "trained to portray a patient in a way that is realistic and repeatable". The five values are safety, quality, professionalism, accountability and collaboration. One domain covers training for role portrayal, feedback and completing assessment instruments. Standardization sits on a spectrum depending on purpose, with tighter standardization for high-stakes assessment. — [Lewis et al., Adv Simul 2017](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s41077-017-0043-4)
- The NBME automated SP evaluation codes each SP response as Correct, Too Much Information, Too Little Information, Incorrect, or Not Applicable. This is a usable rubric for an AI patient. — [Emerson et al., AIME-Con 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.aimecon-main.25.pdf)
- SP feedback forms rate "Consistency in Portrayal" and flag "Over enthusiastic (provides information without being asked)" as a history-taking error. — [Aga Khan University form](https://www.aku.edu:443/mcpk/ded/exam-cell/Documents/SPs%20Feedback%20Form.pdf)
- Portrayals become less congruent when SPs get only basic scenarios with little background on short notice. Character-development training improved congruence. — [AJHPE](https://www.ajol.info/index.php/ajhpe/article/view/104894)
- Trained SPs averaged over 90% accuracy on case specifics over time. [pre-2015] — [Erby et al., PMC3158971](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3158971)
- Differences between individual SPs cause variance in assessment. — [Turner dissertation, core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/217298829.pdf)
- Japan: OSCEs were introduced in 2005. Interview skills were previously a "hidden curriculum". — [BMC Med Educ 2022](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s12909-022-03130-2.pdf)
- Japan: 68 of 80 medical schools answered a national SP-training survey (2011). A Nippon Medical School study found SPs need specific training to answer family-history questions appropriately. — [JNMS](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jnms/80/1/80_57/_article/-char/ja); [Kyoto SP programme](https://cme.med.kyoto-u.ac.jp/en/?p=1775)

### Inferences
- For the AI patient: answer only what is asked, keep a fixed fact sheet so answers stay consistent, give a deep backstory (job, family, beliefs) so improvised answers stay in character, and score turns with the NBME five-category scheme.

### Gaps
- I found no Singapore or Hong Kong SP-programme methodology (e.g. NUS or HKU SP programmes) in this pass.

## 5. Communication pitfalls between students and patients across language and age

### Takeaway
Direct evidence on student-patient pitfalls is thin. The documented risks are language mismatch with elderly patients, depending on family interpreters, and patients disclosing little because of face. Students must actively ask about TCM use and psychosocial factors.

### Cited Findings
- Elderly Singaporeans face significant barriers in English-medium encounters and depend on relatives to interpret. — [NTU chapter](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/oa-edit/10.4324/9781003125815-11/languages-gone-aging-changing-multilingual-linguistic-ecology-ng-bee-chin-francesco-cavallaro); [NTU FYP](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/ntu-10356%2F73494/Description)
- Little conversation-analysis research exists on clinician and elderly-patient interaction in Singapore. NTU ARISE lists it as a 2026-2031 research direction. — [NTU ARISE](https://www.ntu.edu.sg/arise/new-research-directions--2026---2031/social-integration-and-education/quality-healthcare-delivery-for-the-elderly-consent-taking-in-a-cataract-surgery-clinic-in-singapore)
- MOH (2010) said staff get basic Mandarin and dialect courses, and that Malay- and Tamil-speaking colleagues are available. [pre-2015] — [MOH](https://www.moh.gov.sg/news-highlights/details/steps-to-remove-language-barriers)
- Assessment and screening tools developed in Western populations may not be valid for Asian patients. — [Springer 2023 review](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40120-023-00471-y)

### Inferences
- Realistic pitfalls for the simulator: students using jargon ("radiating", "paraesthesia") that elderly personas misread, closed yes/no questions that get a polite "yes" from deference, and students failing to ask about TCM or ointments.

### Gaps
- I found no physiotherapy-specific cross-cultural communication study for SG, HK or JP students, and no Japan-specific data on patient understatement in physio.
